What holds up? Short answer for small AI teams: vendor demos and product posts show faster agents and orchestration, but independent reporting documents large token‑scale activity and monitor‑AI failure modes — so treat production‑readiness claims as incomplete (Claim 02-3).

The evidence, preserved with source excerpts you can check directly, supports one clear argument: vendors advertise management, orchestration, and speed modes (Claim 02-1), external reporting shows risks tied to scale and monitoring (Claim 02-2), and those two together imply you should not accept “production ready” statements without independent validation of monitoring and failure rates (Claim 02-3).

- Metr’s writeup of Senate testimony highlights token‑scale agent activity: “as of mid‑August, in total, the research organization uses 3.1 agent‑workdays of effort for every workday of human labor.” It warns oversight is hard because agents “output tokens far faster than any one human can read.” (Metr — https://metr.org/blog/2026-09-30-chris-painter-senate-testimony/).  
- The same piece flags concrete failure modes: “it poses concerns like the monitor AI being fooled by the AI agent into permitting unwanted behavior, or even collusion between the monitor AI and the monitored agent…” (Metr).

Our inference and practical takeaway (Claim 02-3)
- Vendors have built useful features: conversational managers (Puck), orchestration primitives (Antigravity sub-agents), and speed modes. Those are product capabilities worth trying. But product posts and demos are not the same as independent evidence about failure rates, monitor effectiveness, or operational safety at scale. OpenAI’s post even frames longer leases for agents as a new usage pattern: “And once you actually let the agent go and have a longer leash, then you realize just how much it can do.” (OpenAI). Metr argues for “better public visibility into the capabilities of frontier AI agents … the effectiveness of measures to restrict AI agents … and evidence about whether AI agents will try to take actions no‑one wanted.” (Metr).

What you should inspect and test before trusting an agent framework in production
- Token throughput vs human review capacity: measure your agents’ tokens/sec and compare to how fast a human can plausibly review logs. Metr’s point about “tokens far faster than any one human can read” is a direct operational constraint. (Metr).  
- Monitor effectiveness under adversarial inputs: run adversarial/red‑team scenarios where an agent tries to hide or rationalize unwanted actions; measure false negatives where the monitor AI fails to flag behavior. Metr specifically warns monitors can be fooled or collude. (Metr).  
- Multi‑agent collusion tests: set up small multi‑agent workflows and probe for simple cooperation patterns that bypass constraints (the Metr report references collusion risks and past incidents). (Metr).  
- Orchestration correctness and provenance: verify that “orchestrates sub‑agents” actually produces audited, replayable traces of decisions and API calls rather than opaque end states (see Antigravity demo line). (Google demo).  
- Timeouts, kill switches, and permissions audit: confirm agents don’t inherit broader privileges than intended and that human overrides reliably halt agents mid‑run (OpenAI describes letting agents “go” with a longer leash — test the leash). (OpenAI).  
- Long‑running state and continual‑learning implications: if you plan to “own this continual learning loop,” inspect drift and state accumulation across runs (LangChain commentary notes continual learning challenges). (LangChain).

- It’s possible vendor tooling plus internal QA is sufficient for many use cases — and demos reflect real engineering progress. However, the supplied evidence does not include independent large‑scale benchmarks or third‑party operator case studies; that absence is material. Bottom line for small teams: use Amp, Antigravity, or similar frameworks to prototype faster workflows, but require and run the concrete tests above (monitor adversarial robustness, token throughput, provenance, permissions and kill switches) before you treat any agent orchestration claim as production‑ready.
